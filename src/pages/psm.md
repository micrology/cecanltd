We have been pioneers in applying **Participatory Systems Mapping (PSM)** to appraisal and evaluation. PSM is a method in which a group of stakeholders collaboratively develops a simple causal map of an issue during a workshop.

Stakeholders create a map of factors and links. Factors can represent anything, provided they are expressed as variables that can, in some sense, go up and down. Links represent causal relationships. The map captures what stakeholders believe to be the causal structure of their system. Building it can be hugely valuable to participants; its digitised version is a useful resource, and further analyses can be conducted on the map.

## Applying PSM to policy

PSM is especially relevant to the formulation and evaluation of complex policies: those involving many influential factors, non-linear effects, feedbacks and/or tipping points. Alongside our work on PSM as a methodology, we have developed ideas of **actionable complexity**—finding policy interventions that are effective despite complexity challenges—and methods for policy development in the face of deep uncertainty.

We have developed [software](https://prsm.uk/) for constructing PSM maps online with groups of stakeholders in real time. [PRSM](https://prsm.uk/) is a free, secure, and open-source platform for mapping complex systems.

## Theory of Change and evaluation

A system map can be a precursor to a **Theory of Change**. Formulating a Theory of Change graphically as a system map makes it easier to adapt as we learn more about a domain and as the external environment changes.

The participatory element of PSM is well suited to **developmental evaluation**, as well as more traditional formative and summative evaluation. PSM can also be a valuable ingredient in agile management approaches, where the aim is to “steer” rather than command and control a policy area.

## Analysing system maps

PSM maps can be analysed using **network analysis** tools. We have developed a suite of methods using these tools to identify critical factors, factors vulnerable to shocks, and factors central to the influence network. Our methods recognise that quantitative data on cause and effect is often unavailable or infeasible to collect.

System maps can also provide a starting point for **agent-based models (ABM)**, which simulate scenarios using qualitative and quantitative rules. We have long-standing expertise, dating back thirty years, in constructing ABMs, with recent experience applying them to public policy.

## Understanding wider impacts

Applied to a whole system, these methods can identify interactions between policies and pinpoint unanticipated consequences and secondary impacts that deserve particular attention. They are especially useful when considering cross-cutting policies that may affect several policy teams or span multiple organisations.
